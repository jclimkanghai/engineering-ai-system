# Critical Review Mode

## Purpose

Critical Review Mode is used to deliberately challenge the quality, logic, evidence, assumptions and conclusions of an engineering document review.

Its purpose is not to be negative.

Its purpose is to determine whether a conclusion is:

- supported by evidence;
- logically sound;
- technically defensible;
- contractually justified;
- consistent with other project documents;
- sufficiently complete;
- appropriately qualified; and
- robust against reasonable challenge.

The reviewer shall actively search for weaknesses before accepting a conclusion.

---

## Core Principle

> Do not ask only whether the conclusion could be correct. Ask what evidence would prove it wrong.

A conclusion shall not be accepted merely because:

- it appears reasonable;
- it is common industry practice;
- it has been used on previous projects;
- an experienced engineer would normally assume it;
- another document appears to imply it;
- the contractor could reasonably perform it; or
- no one has raised an objection.

The review must distinguish between:

- **Supported conclusion**
- **Reasonable but unverified conclusion**
- **Conclusion based on assumption**
- **Conclusion contradicted by evidence**
- **Conclusion that cannot presently be determined**

---

# 1. Review Inputs

Use all relevant available evidence, including:

- RFP / RFQ;
- Employer's Requirements;
- Scope of Work;
- Technical Specifications;
- Tender Bulletins;
- Tender Clarifications;
- Addenda;
- Contract conditions;
- BOQ;
- Schedules;
- Drawings;
- Drawing registers;
- Design Basis;
- Design Reports;
- Geotechnical Reports;
- Survey information;
- Metocean data;
- Existing-condition information;
- Regulatory requirements;
- Authority approvals;
- Codes and standards;
- Method statements;
- Programme information;
- Correspondence;
- Meeting minutes;
- Transmittals;
- referenced documents; and
- other project evidence.

The reviewer shall consider document hierarchy and revision status before relying on information.

---

# 2. Critical Review Sequence

For every significant conclusion:

1. Identify the conclusion.
2. Identify the evidence supporting it.
3. Identify the source and revision.
4. Check whether the source is authoritative.
5. Check whether the source is current.
6. Check for contradictory evidence.
7. Check whether important evidence is missing.
8. Identify assumptions.
9. Test the underlying logic.
10. Consider alternative interpretations.
11. Test the strongest opposing argument.
12. Determine whether the conclusion remains defensible.
13. Assess materiality and risk.
14. Recommend the appropriate action.

---

# 3. Evidence Challenge

For each material statement ask:

### Evidence existence

- Is there explicit evidence?
- Is the statement inferred?
- Is it based on industry practice?
- Is it based on engineering judgement?
- Is it merely assumed?

### Evidence quality

- Is the source contractual?
- Is the source technical?
- Is the source regulatory?
- Is the source informational?
- Is the source preliminary?
- Is the source superseded?

### Evidence completeness

- Is the evidence sufficient to support the conclusion?
- Is critical information missing?
- Is only one source being considered when multiple sources exist?
- Has the relevant drawing, specification, bulletin or clarification been checked?

### Evidence consistency

- Do other documents support the same conclusion?
- Is there contradictory information?
- Has a later revision changed the requirement?

---

# 4. Logic Challenge

Test whether the reasoning follows logically from the evidence.

Identify:

- unsupported leaps;
- circular reasoning;
- hidden assumptions;
- false equivalence;
- incomplete reasoning;
- incorrect causation;
- over-generalisation;
- ambiguous terminology;
- incorrect interpretation of contractual language;
- inappropriate extrapolation from another project;
- inappropriate extrapolation from industry practice.

Do not accept:

> "This is normally done this way."

as sufficient justification for:

> "Therefore this is a project requirement."

Industry practice may support engineering judgement but does not automatically establish a contractual obligation.

---

# 5. Alternative Interpretation Test

For every material interpretation:

1. Identify the proposed interpretation.
2. Identify at least one reasonable alternative interpretation where applicable.
3. Compare the supporting evidence.
4. Determine which interpretation has stronger documentary support.
5. Identify what information would resolve the uncertainty.

Use the following classifications:

- **Clearly supported**
- **Preferred interpretation**
- **Two or more reasonable interpretations**
- **Weakly supported**
- **Unsupported**
- **Not determinable**

Do not manufacture alternative interpretations when the document is unambiguous.

---

# 6. Strongest Counterargument

For significant findings, deliberately construct the strongest reasonable counterargument.

Ask:

> If someone challenged this finding during tender clarification, technical adjudication, contract negotiation, design review or dispute resolution, what would their strongest argument be?

Consider:

- contractual wording;
- document hierarchy;
- later revisions;
- implied scope;
- performance requirements;
- referenced standards;
- drawings;
- BOQ descriptions;
- responsibility matrices;
- construction obligations;
- regulatory obligations;
- normal contractor obligations;
- design development obligations.

Then determine whether the original finding survives the challenge.

---
